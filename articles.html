India's Report Card – 2026: Vishwaguru? Not Yet.

Beyond slogans and economic headlines, what do the numbers tell us about India's democracy, citizens' well-being, environment and prosperity?

By Sambit Saha | The Rethinkers Journal | 2026
"Slogans don't change the numbers. The world is keeping score."

Introduction: The Vishwaguru Question

India has long been associated with the idea of becoming a Vishwaguru — a teacher, guide or source of knowledge for the world. It is an ambitious vision rooted in India's civilisational history, intellectual traditions, cultural influence and growing international presence.

In recent years, India's economic expansion, technological progress, diplomatic influence and infrastructure development have strengthened its position on the global stage. As one of the world's largest economies and most populous nations, India has considerable potential to shape the future of international affairs.

But there is an important question that deserves attention: can international leadership be measured only through economic size, military capability and global influence?

A country's development must also be evaluated through the conditions experienced by its citizens. The strength of democratic institutions, quality of life, environmental safety, economic opportunity and accountability of public authorities are equally important indicators.

India's Report Card – 2026 examines several international indicators and a controversy surrounding the Election Commission of India. Together, these subjects raise a broader question about the difference between a country's aspirations and its measurable achievements.

The central argument: A country aspiring to lead the world must demonstrate progress not only in its international standing, but also in the everyday lives of its people and the institutions responsible for protecting their rights.

1. Democracy: The Strength of Institutions

0.38 Score shown in the infographic for the specified V-Dem index

Democracy is one of the foundations of a modern constitutional state. It provides citizens with the ability to participate in governance, choose their representatives and hold those in power accountable.

However, democracy is not defined simply by the existence of elections. A country may conduct elections regularly while facing difficulties involving political competition, civil liberties, institutional independence or freedom of expression.

The V-Dem Institute's Democracy Report 2026 classifies India as an electoral autocracy since 2017. This is the institute's academic classification based on its own framework and indicators. It is a serious assessment, although it should not be confused with a legal declaration that India is a dictatorship.

What is an electoral autocracy?

An electoral autocracy is a political system in which elections take place, but the democratic standards necessary for meaningful political competition and accountability are not adequately maintained.

In a healthy democracy, several conditions must exist together:

  • Free and fair elections.
  • Genuine competition between political parties.
  • Independence of constitutional institutions.
  • Freedom of expression and a free press.
  • Protection of civil liberties.
  • An independent judiciary and rule of law.
  • Accountability of elected representatives.

The important distinction is that elections provide citizens with a mechanism to select their representatives, while democratic institutions ensure that political power remains accountable.

The V-Dem ranking and score displayed in the infographic should always be identified with the exact index being used. Different democracy indices measure different dimensions, and a single number cannot describe every aspect of a country's political system.

Why does this matter?

Democracy is not merely about who wins an election. It is also about whether citizens can question those in power, whether institutions can operate independently and whether political opponents can compete on fair terms.

The quality of a democracy is ultimately reflected in the strength of its institutions and the rights enjoyed by its citizens.

2. Happiness: Economic Growth and the Quality of Life

99 / 147 Nepal – World Happiness Report 2026
104 / 147 Pakistan – World Happiness Report 2026

Economic growth is often used as a measure of national progress. However, a country's economic performance does not automatically translate into a higher quality of life for every citizen.

The World Happiness Report 2026 places India at 116th among 147 countries. Nepal ranks 99th, while Pakistan ranks 104th. These positions invite an important discussion about how people evaluate their own lives.

What does the World Happiness Report measure?

The report does not simply count how often people smile or how frequently they describe themselves as happy. Its main country rankings are based on people's evaluations of their own lives.

Respondents use the Cantril Ladder, a scale from 0 to 10, to evaluate where they believe their lives currently stand. The rankings generally use three years of survey responses to improve reliability.

Researchers also examine factors associated with these evaluations, including:

  1. GDP per capita: The economic resources available to people.
  2. Social support: Whether individuals have someone to rely on.
  3. Healthy life expectancy: Expected years of healthy life.
  4. Freedom: People's perceived freedom to make life choices.
  5. Generosity: Patterns of charitable behaviour.
  6. Perceptions of corruption: How people view corruption in institutions.

The happiness paradox

A nation can experience improvements in infrastructure, technology, industrial production and international trade while many of its citizens continue to face concerns about employment, income security, healthcare, education and social support.

This does not mean that a single happiness ranking explains every social condition in a country. It does, however, remind us that development should be evaluated through people's lived experiences, not only through national economic statistics.

Economic growth matters, but the ultimate purpose of economic development should be to improve human lives.

3. Pollution: The Cost of Development

66 / 100 Of the world's 100 most polluted cities were in India in the IQAir 2025 report
48.9 µg/m³ India's population-weighted annual PM2.5 average in 2025

Economic development and industrial expansion are important for improving living standards. But development that ignores environmental consequences can create long-term problems for public health and the sustainability of cities.

The IQAir World Air Quality Report 2025 presents a concerning picture of air pollution in India. According to the report, 66 of the world's 100 most polluted cities were located in India.

India's population-weighted annual average PM2.5 concentration was reported at 48.9 micrograms per cubic metre. This is almost ten times the World Health Organization's annual guideline of 5 micrograms per cubic metre.

Understanding PM2.5

PM2.5 refers to fine particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometres or less. These particles are small enough to penetrate deep into the respiratory system and are associated with significant health risks.

The World Health Organization's 2021 Air Quality Guidelines recommend the following PM2.5 limits:

  • Annual average: 5 µg/m³.
  • 24-hour average: 15 µg/m³.

What contributes to air pollution?

Air pollution in Indian cities is influenced by several sources, often operating together:

  • Vehicle emissions and traffic congestion.
  • Industrial emissions and coal-based power generation.
  • Construction and road dust.
  • Burning of agricultural residues.
  • Waste burning and certain household fuels.
  • Weather conditions that restrict the movement of pollutants.

Delhi was ranked the world's most polluted capital in the 2025 IQAir report, with an annual average PM2.5 concentration of 82.2 µg/m³. It was the seventh time in eight years of IQAir reporting that Delhi held this position.

Why is this a national concern?

Pollution is not simply an environmental statistic. It affects public health, educational attendance, productivity, quality of life and the long-term sustainability of urban development.

A country aspiring to become a global leader must also consider the environment in which its citizens live. Cleaner air and sustainable cities are not optional luxuries; they are essential components of meaningful development.

4. Per-Capita Income: A Large Economy, Unequal Prosperity?

India is among the world's largest economies. Yet the size of a country's total economy does not necessarily tell us how much economic output is associated with each individual.

What is GDP per capita?

GDP per capita is calculated by dividing a country's total Gross Domestic Product by its population.

GDP per capita = Total GDP ÷ Total Population

It is commonly used to compare average economic output per person across countries. However, it does not represent the actual salary, wealth or disposable income received by every citizen.

IMF estimates for 2026

The October 2026 edition of the International Monetary Fund's World Economic Outlook provides the following estimates for nominal GDP per capita at current prices:

Country GDP per capita (USD)
United States $95,210
Bangladesh $2,890
India $2,750

These estimates place Bangladesh marginally ahead of India in this particular nominal GDP-per-capita comparison. The United States' figure is approximately 34.6 times India's figure.

Why should we look beyond total GDP?

A country's total GDP measures the value of goods and services produced within its economy. It is an important indicator of economic scale, but it does not tell us how evenly the benefits of growth are distributed.

GDP per capita provides another perspective, although it too has limitations. It is an average and can hide significant differences in income and wealth between individuals.

Therefore, a complete assessment of economic prosperity should also consider income distribution, employment, wages, access to essential services, purchasing power and poverty.

Important distinction: Nominal GDP per capita, purchasing-power-parity GDP per capita and average personal income are different measures. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

5. The SIR Controversy: Questions Within the Election Commission

One of the most significant institutional controversies highlighted in the infographic concerns the Election Commission of India and the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls, commonly known as SIR.

What is SIR?

Special Intensive Revision is an exercise conducted by the Election Commission to review and update electoral rolls. Its purpose is to maintain accurate voter lists and ensure that eligible citizens can participate in elections.

Electoral-roll revision can involve:

  • Adding eligible new voters.
  • Removing duplicate entries.
  • Removing entries of deceased voters.
  • Updating incorrect personal information.
  • Addressing changes of residence.
  • Verifying voter eligibility under applicable rules.

The exercise itself is not inherently controversial. The important questions concern its implementation, verification procedures, transparency and the protection of eligible voters from wrongful exclusion.

The reported objections by two Election Commissioners

An investigation published by The Indian Express on 23 September 2026 reported that two of the three Election Commissioners had formally objected to decisions and actions connected with the electoral-roll process at least 14 times over approximately ten months.

The two commissioners identified in the report were:

  • Sukhbir Singh Sandhu
  • Vivek Joshi

The third member of the Commission is Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar.

According to the investigation, the objections concerned decisions that the two commissioners said had been taken without adequate consultation, knowledge or approval.

What were the concerns?

A. Questions about collective decision-making

The commissioners reportedly raised concerns about decisions and administrative actions being issued in the name of the Commission without sufficient involvement of all its members.

B. Changes involving voter registration

The investigation reported objections to changes involving Form 6, which is used for new voter registration. The commissioners reportedly questioned the legal basis and authorisation for certain changes.

C. Centralisation of electoral-roll data

Another reported concern involved the centralisation of electoral-roll databases and digital systems. Questions were raised about the access and authority available to state-level election officials.

D. Transparency and accountability

Electoral databases are important because errors in voter records can affect citizens' ability to exercise their voting rights. Their management therefore requires clear procedures, accurate information and appropriate accountability.

The Election Commission's response

The Election Commission responded to the reporting by acknowledging the existence of the notes but describing them as suggestions forming part of its decision-making process. It maintained that decisions, including those concerning SIR, had been unanimous.

The Commission's response and the investigation's findings should both be considered when examining this controversy.

A necessary distinction: The reported objections are serious matters of institutional concern. However, they should not be presented as a final judicial finding that elections were manipulated or that the Commission committed proven wrongdoing. Allegations, internal disagreements and legally established facts are different categories of evidence.

Why does this matter?

The Election Commission is a constitutional institution entrusted with supervising elections in India. Its credibility depends on public confidence in its independence, transparency and impartiality.

Citizens must be able to trust that eligible voters will not be unfairly excluded, electoral records will be accurately maintained and decisions will follow the law.

When disagreements emerge within an institution responsible for protecting the electoral process, they deserve careful scrutiny and clear explanations.

6. Elections Alone Do Not Define Democracy

"Not the absence of elections — the absence of fair elections."

The existence of elections is essential to representative democracy, but elections alone are not sufficient to establish that a political system is functioning democratically.

A meaningful democratic system requires citizens to have a genuine opportunity to participate in political life and hold those in power accountable.

This requires several conditions:

  1. Free elections: Citizens must be able to vote without coercion or intimidation.
  2. Fair competition: Political parties must have a genuine opportunity to compete.
  3. Independent institutions: Constitutional authorities must be able to function impartially.
  4. Freedom of expression: Citizens must be able to question and criticise public authorities.
  5. Press freedom: Journalists must be able to investigate and report independently.
  6. Rule of law: Government authorities must remain subject to the Constitution and applicable laws.
  7. Accountability: Elected representatives must answer to the public.

Political scientists use different categories to describe political systems, including liberal democracies, electoral democracies and electoral autocracies. These classifications are based on different institutional and political criteria.

It is important to use these terms accurately. Not every democratic weakness automatically makes a country a dictatorship. At the same time, the presence of elections should not prevent citizens from examining the quality of the institutions surrounding them.

8. What Should Global Leadership Actually Mean?

The term Vishwaguru carries an aspiration much larger than becoming economically powerful or diplomatically influential. It suggests the ability to provide knowledge, guidance and meaningful contributions to the wider world.

India's history offers significant contributions to mathematics, philosophy, medicine, literature, astronomy and numerous other fields. Its cultural heritage and intellectual traditions are important parts of its global identity.

But historical achievements alone cannot determine the quality of a modern nation. Leadership must also be demonstrated through present-day institutions, public policy and the everyday conditions experienced by citizens.

A country aspiring to lead by example should work towards:

  • A democratic system with strong and accountable institutions.
  • Greater access to quality education and healthcare.
  • Economic opportunities and broad-based prosperity.
  • Clean air, safe water and sustainable cities.
  • Transparent electoral processes.
  • Scientific research, innovation and technological advancement.
  • Equal protection of rights and the rule of law.

These objectives are not mutually exclusive. Economic development can support better public services, while strong institutions can create the conditions necessary for sustainable economic progress.

The challenge is to ensure that national ambition translates into measurable improvements in the lives of ordinary people.

Conclusion: Leadership Begins at Home

India's potential is undeniable. Its population, cultural heritage, scientific capabilities, entrepreneurial energy and growing international influence provide a strong foundation for future progress.

But potential and achievement are not the same thing. A nation's aspirations must be supported by institutions that function effectively, an environment that protects public health, an economy that creates wider opportunities and a political system that remains accountable to its citizens.

The figures discussed here should not be treated as a complete description of India. Nor should criticism of public institutions be confused with criticism of the country or its people. Constructive criticism is an essential part of a society that wants to improve.

The real question is not whether India has the potential to become a global leader. It is whether that potential is being translated into a better, fairer and more sustainable society.

Because a Vishwaguru does not become one simply by declaring itself a teacher. It earns that position through knowledge, integrity, achievement and the example it sets for others.

A Vishwaguru first teaches by example.
